# A childhood library of folktales from around the world

## Working source guide for a readable, illustrated collection

There is a particular pleasure in encountering the world through a shelf of storybooks: clever rabbits and impossible tasks, haunted roads, talking fish, mountain spirits, ungrateful tigers, youngest children, old women with gifts, and doors that should not be opened. This guide is a starting shelf for building a new, easily readable collection from public-domain sources.

The aim is not to flatten many cultures into one generic book of “world tales.” Each story should retain a specific place, language or community where the source supports one, while remaining inviting to a general reader. Historical illustrations can help recover the feeling of the old books many of us read as children—but their rights and cultural framing need to be checked separately from the stories.

## A good first shelf

These are practical source editions: generally searchable, downloadable, and old enough to be strong public-domain candidates. Project Gutenberg labels the listed editions public domain in the United States; publication elsewhere requires a jurisdiction-specific check.

### Korea

- [*Korean Tales*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/55539), Horace Newton Allen (1889). An especially useful narrative spine: animal legends, *The Enchanted Wine Jug*, *Heungbu and Nolbu* (printed as “Hyung Bo and Nahl Bo”), *Chunhyang*, *Sim Cheong*, and *Hong Gildong*. The prose is relatively approachable, but names should be given modern romanizations with the historical forms retained in source notes.
- [*Korean Folk Tales: Imps, Ghosts and Fairies*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/51002), translated by James S. Gale from Im Bang and Yi Ryuk (1913). Strong material for ghosts, fox spirits, geomancy, seers, strange transformations, and the supernatural. Better suited to older children and adults than a uniformly young audience.
- [*Korean Fairy Tales*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/67180), William Elliot Griffis (1922). Twenty-six accessible stories involving dokkaebi (printed “Tokgabi”), tigers, mountain fairies, magical animals, and moral reversals. The digitized edition includes illustrations and is a promising visual source.

Editorial note: these English editions were mediated by Western collectors or translators. Preserve the tale, but verify names and context against modern Korean scholarship. Do not reproduce their generalizations about Korea uncritically.

### East and Southeast Asia

- China: E. T. C. Werner’s *Myths and Legends of China*, available through the [Project Gutenberg folklore shelf](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/bookshelf/37). Useful for mythic figures and longer explanatory entries. Also consider *The Chinese Fairy Book* by Richard Wilhelm in an edition whose translator and illustrator rights have been cleared.
- Japan: Yei Theodora Ozaki’s *Japanese Fairy Tales* and Mitford’s *Tales of Old Japan*, both listed on the [Gutenberg folklore shelf](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/bookshelf/37). Ozaki is the more immediately child-readable starting point. Lafcadio Hearn’s *Kwaidan* can supply atmospheric supernatural stories for older readers.
- Philippines: *Philippine Folk Tales*, *Philippine Folk-Tales*, and *Filipino Popular Tales* appear in [Gutenberg’s folklore catalogue](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/bookshelf/37?sort_order=title&start_index=26). Record the named ethnolinguistic group and island or region whenever the original collector provides them; “Filipino” is too broad as final metadata.

### South Asia

- [Project Gutenberg’s India tales catalogue](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/subject/926) brings together *Folklore of the Santal Parganas*, *Santal Folk Tales*, *Tales of the Sun; or, Folklore of Southern India*, *Folk-Tales of Bengal*, *Indian Fairy Tales*, *Tales of the Punjab*, and several classical story cycles.
- Somadeva’s *Kathāsaritsāgara* and the *Vetāla* story cycle are valuable for nested and linked tales, but historical English translations need more editing than the shorter collections.
- For an initial child-friendly selection, begin with *Tales of the Sun*, *Folk-Tales of Bengal*, and a carefully reviewed selection of Santal stories. Identify colonial terminology in source notes rather than silently carrying it into modern copy.

### Middle East and neighbouring traditions

- The [Arab folklore catalogue at Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/subject/1921) contains several historical versions of *The Arabian Nights*, including editions intended for younger readers. Treat “Arabian Nights” as a transmitted, multilingual story complex rather than the folklore of one homogeneous culture.
- Wikisource’s [folklore portal](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Portal:Folklore) points to the *Book of Dede Korkut*, an important Turkic/Oghuz epic tradition. Confirm the rights in the particular English translation, not merely the medieval underlying work.
- Persian, Turkish, Armenian, Kurdish, Jewish, and other regional traditions deserve their own source tracks as the collection grows rather than being absorbed into an “Arabian” category.

### Africa

- [West African folklore at Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/subject/28313): *West African Folk-Tales* and *Cunnie Rabbit, Mr. Spider and the Other Beef*. These provide lively animal and trickster material, though historical collectors’ labels and spellings should be checked.
- [*South-African Folk-Tales*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/38339), James A. Honey. Includes animal tales such as “The Story of Lion and Little Jackal.” Preserve the source’s location and attribution data, but replace the blanket colonial-era regional label with more precise community information where it can be responsibly established.
- *Where Animals Talk: West African Folk-Lore Tales* is listed in [Gutenberg’s folklore catalogue](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/results/?lang=en&locc=GR&pageno=2) and is worth reviewing as a second West African source.

Africa should not become one anthology section. A later source pass should deliberately seek North African, Horn of Africa, Central African, East African, southern African, Malagasy, and multiple West African language traditions.

### Northern Europe

- Norway: [Gutenberg’s Norwegian folklore catalogue](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/subject/16757) includes *East of the Sun and West of the Moon*, *Popular Tales from the Norse*, and *Tales from the Fjeld*. These offer trolls, quests, transformations, and wonderfully visual landscapes.
- Finland: [Gutenberg’s Finnish folklore catalogue](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/subject/5854) includes *Mighty Mikko*, *Finnish Legends for English Children*, and the *Kalevala*. Use short folk narratives first; treat the *Kalevala* as an edited epic compilation rather than a bag of interchangeable fairy tales.
- Iceland: the sagas and later elf or troll legends should be separate strands. Gutenberg’s broad [mythology and folklore catalogue](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/bookshelf/646?sort_order=author&start_index=3251) includes *The Story of Burnt Njal* and *Eirik the Red’s Saga*.
- Denmark: Hans Christian Andersen is literary fairy tale rather than anonymous folklore. Include him only under a clearly labelled “literary tales” branch, not as direct evidence of oral tradition.

### Celtic-language and insular European traditions

- Scotland: [*The Scottish Fairy Book*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/37532), Elizabeth W. Grierson, illustrated by Morris Meredith Williams. A particularly attractive candidate for preserving the childhood-storybook feeling: brownies, selkies, Thomas the Rhymer, *The Black Bull of Norroway*, *Katherine Crackernuts*, and other tales, with historical illustrations.
- Wales: *The Welsh Fairy Book*, *Welsh Folk-Lore*, and the *Mabinogion* are starting points. Gutenberg’s [folklore class catalogue](https://www.gutenberg.org/browse/loccs/gr) lists regional Welsh collections. Keep medieval literary material distinct from later collected folk belief.
- Ireland: James Stephens’s *Irish Fairy Tales* is on the [Gutenberg folklore shelf](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/bookshelf/37). Joseph Jacobs, Douglas Hyde, Lady Wilde, and W. B. Yeats offer further historical collections, subject to edition-level checks.
- Cornwall, Brittany, and the Isle of Man should eventually receive their own entries rather than being folded into an undifferentiated “Celtic” identity.

### Central, Eastern, and Southeastern Europe

- Russia: *Russian Fairy Tales: A Choice Collection of Muscovite Folk-lore* and *More Russian Picture Tales* are prominent illustrated or illustration-friendly sources on the [Gutenberg folklore shelf](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/bookshelf/37).
- Broader Slavic traditions: *Fairy Tales of the Slav Peasants and Herdsmen* and *Serbian Fairy Tales* provide an opening, but stories should be assigned to a specific language or source community where possible.
- Poland, Czechia, Slovakia, Ukraine, Belarus, Bulgaria, Croatia, Slovenia, Serbia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro, and North Macedonia merit separate searches. Modern borders should not be retroactively imposed without explanation.
- Romania: seek public-domain English translations of Petre Ispirescu and carefully document the translator. Do not assume that the underlying tale’s age makes a modern translation reusable.
- Hungary: historical “Hungarian Fairy Book” editions can be useful, but collector, translator, and illustrator dates must all be checked.
- Baltic region: build distinct Estonian, Latvian, and Lithuanian tracks. Their traditions should not be filed as either generically Nordic or Slavic.

### Western and Southern Europe

- German-speaking traditions: Grimm’s *Household Tales* is foundational, but specify the edition and translator. Some editions contain famous public-domain illustrations by artists such as Walter Crane or Arthur Rackham; each artist requires a separate rights record.
- France: Charles Perrault’s tales are literary versions with a long publication history. Keep Perrault’s wording and later oral variants conceptually separate.
- Italy: Giambattista Basile, Straparola, and Thomas Frederick Crane’s *Italian Popular Tales* provide several layers of literary and collected tradition.
- Spain and Portugal: begin with named regional collections rather than one Iberian bucket, retaining distinctions among Castilian, Catalan, Galician, Basque, Portuguese, and other sources.
- Greece: separate ancient mythology, medieval material, and modern collected folktales. “Greek myth” should not crowd later folk traditions off the shelf.

### The Americas and Pacific

This part of the project requires the most care about living communities and cultural authority.

- The Library of Congress [American Folklife Center](https://www.loc.gov/research-centers/american-folklife-center/collections/) holds materials from the United States and around the world. Rights vary by collection and item.
- Historical books such as *Stories the Iroquois Tell Their Children*, *American Indian Stories* by Zitkala-Ša, and *Folk-Lore and Legends: North American Indian* appear on the [Gutenberg folklore shelf](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/bookshelf/37). Their legal availability does not make every reuse culturally appropriate, and generic or outdated labels must not be retained as the project’s own taxonomy.
- For Indigenous material, consult community-originated resources and [Traditional Knowledge labels](https://memory.loc.gov/collections/ancestral-voices/about-this-collection/rights-and-access/). A work can be legally public domain while carrying community expectations about attribution, sacred knowledge, seasonal use, gendered knowledge, or commercial reuse.
- Build separate future source lists for First Nations, Inuit, Métis, Native American, Caribbean, Mesoamerican, South American, Hawaiian, Māori, Pasifika, and Aboriginal Australian traditions. Do not publish sacred or restricted material merely because an old collector printed it.

## Visual source strategy

Illustrations should be treated as first-class records, not decorations detached from their books.

For each image, save:

- image title or the story/page it illustrates;
- artist, engraver, and photographer where applicable;
- source book, publisher, year, and scan provider;
- stable source URL and page number;
- the artist’s death year and the relevant rights conclusion;
- notes about stereotyped imagery, inaccurate costume, or colonial framing;
- whether the image will be reproduced, restored, cropped, recoloured, or used only as visual research.

Good first illustrated candidates include *Korean Fairy Tales*, *The Scottish Fairy Book*, *East of the Sun and West of the Moon*, *More Russian Picture Tales*, Ozaki’s Japanese collections, selected Grimm editions, and historical Aesop editions. The Library of Congress explicitly identifies its 1919 Milo Winter edition of [*The Aesop for Children*](https://www.loc.gov/collections/childrens-book-selections/articles-and-essays/aesop-for-children-interactive-book/) as public domain in the United States.

Avoid lifting an image from a modern reprint or blog when the underlying old illustration is public domain. Locate and cite the original scan instead. A modern scan may also come with site terms even where the pictured work itself is public domain.

## Proposed record for every story

```yaml
title_display: The Enchanted Wine Jug
title_source: The Enchanted Wine Jug
title_original: null
tradition: Korean
language_of_tale: Korean

source_book: Korean Tales
source_year: 1889

source_pages: null
edition_rights_us: public domain per Project Gutenberg
edition_rights_canada: review completed / pending
illustrator: null
illustration_rights: not applicable / review completed / pending
retelling_author: project contributor
retelling_license: CC0 or chosen open licence
age_band: 8+
content_notes: null
community_or_tk_notes: none found / review required
editorial_notes: modernize romanization; verify cultural context
```

Also track motifs, story type, reading time, tone, ending type, content notes, and related variants—but do not let classification replace cultural context.

## Rights workflow for publication from Canada

Copyright attaches to the particular expression: the collector’s prose, a translator’s English wording, an illustrator’s images, editorial notes, and a modern book design can each have different rights. The traditional plot or character may be free to retell while a recent translation is protected.

Canada generally protects a work for the author’s life plus 70 years. The Canadian Intellectual Property Office notes that the 2022 extension from life-plus-50 did **not** revive works already in the public domain before January 1, 2023; it also notes that exceptions exist. See the official [CIPO guide to copyright](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canadian-intellectual-property-office/en/guide-copyright).

For every candidate edition:

1. Identify all relevant creators: author or collector, translator, illustrator, and sometimes editor.
2. Record each creator’s death year from a reliable authority record.
3. Determine status in Canada and in every primary publication market; do not copy Gutenberg’s US conclusion into the Canadian field.
4. Save evidence: catalogue record, title-page scan, publication date, creator authority record, and the repository’s rights statement.
5. If rights remain uncertain, use the old source only as research and write a genuinely new retelling without copying its distinctive language.
6. Apply a clear licence to the project’s new text and original art. CC0 maximizes reuse; CC BY preserves an attribution requirement.

This document is a research guide, not a legal opinion.

## Editorial principles

- Name the culture, language, community, and region as precisely as the evidence permits.
- Make clear whether a piece is an oral folktale, myth, legend, epic episode, literary fairy tale, collector’s adaptation, or modern retelling.
- Preserve variant titles and historical spellings as searchable aliases, not necessarily as display text.
- Credit known tellers and source communities—not only the person who wrote the story down.
- Write new, lucid retellings from compared sources instead of merely simplifying one historical translator sentence by sentence.
- Do not “correct” unfamiliar moral structures into contemporary Western lessons.
- Flag violence and frightening material by age band rather than bleaching every story into nursery prose.
- Keep provenance visible to the reader through a short source note on every story.
- Invite cultural readers and scholars to review context and pronunciation, and pay them when the project has a budget.
- Treat public domain as a legal floor, not the full measure of respectful use.

## A manageable pilot

A first volume or site section could contain 24 stories—two each from twelve well-sourced traditions—with a mix of animal tales, comic tales, wonder tales, ghosts, quests, and legends. Korea should be represented by more than a single “representative” story; two contrasting pieces, such as a comic tiger or dokkaebi tale and one of the classic narrative cycles, would immediately show greater range.

For every pilot story, publish:

- a five-to-fifteen-minute retelling;
- one cleared historical or newly commissioned illustration;
- a short cultural note written with restraint;
- pronunciation help where useful;
- the exact source edition and a link to the scan;
- a plain-language rights and adaptation statement;
- links to one or two variants for curious readers.

## Source hubs

- [Project Gutenberg folklore shelf](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/bookshelf/37) — the fastest broad discovery point for downloadable historical texts.
- [Project Gutenberg folklore classification](https://www.gutenberg.org/browse/loccs/gr) — a larger author-and-title index useful for deeper searches.
- [Wikisource folklore portal](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Portal:Folklore) — transcribed historical works; check whether each item is public domain or CC BY-SA and whether that status applies in the publication country.
- [Wikisource copyright policy](https://wikisource.org/wiki/Wikisource:Copyright_policy) — explains the repository’s public-domain and free-licence requirements, including translation issues.
- [Library of Congress American Folklife Center](https://www.loc.gov/research-centers/american-folklife-center/collections/) — rich primary documentation with collection-specific rights and ethical conditions.
- [Library of Congress Free to Use and Reuse](https://www.loc.gov/free-to-use/) — curated visual material, with a reminder to consult each collection’s rights statement.
- [Internet Sacred Text Archive](https://archive.sacred-texts.com/volun.htm) — a large discovery archive for mythology, religion, and folklore containing both public-domain and copyrighted-but-redistributable texts; verify each item rather than assuming the whole site is public domain.
- [Canadian Intellectual Property Office copyright guide](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canadian-intellectual-property-office/en/guide-copyright) — the baseline Canadian term and public-domain guidance.

## Reading format research: an iPad-like experience for children

### Recommendation

Build the primary reader as a responsive, HTML-first progressive web app (PWA), then generate a reflowable EPUB 3 from the same story source. Add a fixed-layout rendition only for stories in which the relationship between words and a full-page illustration is essential.

This gives the project a low-friction link children can open in Safari, an installable home-screen experience with offline reading, and a portable book file for Apple Books and other EPUB readers. It also avoids making every story conform to a single tablet size.

### Format comparison

| Format | Strengths for this project | Limitations | Recommended role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Responsive HTML/PWA | Opens from a link; full control of large touch controls, narration, vocabulary help, progress, themes, and offline caching; updates without redownloading a book | Offline behavior and storage need careful Safari testing; home-screen installation is less obvious than installing an app; custom pagination requires engineering | Primary reading experience |
| Reflowable EPUB 3 | Portable single file; supported by Apple Books and other reading systems; adjustable text; semantic HTML; supports accessibility metadata and media overlays | Reader behavior and CSS support vary; custom interactions should remain modest | Download/archive edition |
| Fixed-layout EPUB 3 | Preserves designed spreads and full-bleed illustration placement; Apple explicitly recommends it for highly illustrated children’s books; can support timed read-aloud | Text resizing and reflow are constrained; expensive to adapt across screens and languages; one XHTML document is commonly required per fixed page | Optional picture-book edition |
| PDF | Preserves printed pages and is easy to produce | Poor text reflow, weak small-screen adaptation, and less suitable for an interactive child reader | Facsimile/source download only |
| Readium Web Publication | Defines a publication, reading order, resources, and metadata over HTTPS; provides a path between EPUB assets and a browser reader | Adds architecture and tooling complexity; Readium Web still describes itself as work in progress | Later interoperability layer, not MVP |

EPUB 3 is itself a package of web technologies—HTML/XHTML, CSS, SVG, metadata, and related assets—so an HTML-first content model is not a dead end. The [W3C EPUB 3 overview](https://www.w3.org/TR/epub-overview-34/) confirms support for both reflowable and fixed-layout publications and for media overlays that synchronize recorded narration with highlighted text. Apple’s [book-design guidance](https://authors.apple.com/support/3975-designing-great-book-layouts-interiors) recommends fixed layout for children’s books dominated by illustrations, while its [format guide](https://help.apple.com/itc/applebooksstoreformatting/en.lproj/static.html) identifies “Read Aloud” as timed audio in fixed-layout EPUB.

### Suggested reading interaction

Use one continuous story document in the data model, divided into meaningful scenes. Present those scenes as calm, screen-sized cards on a tablet:

- swipe horizontally or tap generous left/right zones to move between scenes;
- allow vertical scrolling within a scene when larger text makes it necessary—never clip text to preserve a fake page;
- reveal controls on a single tap and keep them out of the reading area otherwise;
- offer text size, line spacing, light/dark/warm themes, and narration speed behind one obvious “Aa” control;
- save position locally without requiring an account;
- include a visible table of contents and “return to library” control;
- let a child tap an unfamiliar name for pronunciation or a short definition without leaving the story;
- avoid gamification, streaks, pop-ups, autoplay, and links that unexpectedly leave the reader.

CSS scroll snap can create a book-like gesture, but it should be progressive enhancement rather than the content model. Normal scrolling must remain a fallback. A “page” should be a navigable scene or section, not text cut at a fixed character count; font scaling, translation, portrait/landscape rotation, and Split View all change how much fits.

### Two visual modes from one story

**Reading mode** should be reflowable: live HTML text, an illustration placed between paragraphs or at scene boundaries, and user-controlled type. This is the default for independent readers and accessibility.

**Read-together mode** can resemble a picture book: one illustration-led scene per screen, shorter visible passages, optional recorded narration, and gentle word or sentence highlighting. The underlying words must remain real HTML text rather than being baked into an image.

This dual-mode approach preserves the magic of an illustrated childhood book without sacrificing legibility. Historical art can be cropped responsively in reading mode while an uncropped, zoomable view preserves the artifact.

### Narration and read-along

Prefer human narration for a curated pilot. Store timings at sentence or phrase level so the reader can highlight the current passage without flashing word by word. A play action should always begin with a deliberate tap: Safari restricts unprompted media playback, and unexpected speech is poor child-facing behavior regardless.

For export, the same timing data can generate EPUB Media Overlays. W3C specifically describes synchronized highlighting and recorded narration as useful for learning to read. Provide pause, replay sentence, narration speed, and “read myself” modes, and ensure the story remains complete without audio.

Do not make browser text-to-speech the only narration path. Voices and pronunciation vary across devices, particularly for Korean and other non-English names. Include language tags (`lang`) around original-language words so speech engines and screen readers have a better chance of pronouncing them correctly.

### Child-centred accessibility baseline

- Use semantic landmarks, headings, paragraphs, lists, and buttons; maintain a logical reading order.
- Keep text as text. W3C’s [EPUB accessibility techniques](https://www.w3.org/TR/epub-a11y-tech-11/) caution that images of characters or words impair text-to-speech even when alt text exists.
- Supply useful alt text for narrative illustrations and empty alt text for purely decorative ornaments. Offer a longer description when an image contains story information not stated in the prose.
- Support text enlargement without loss of content. Fixed-layout designs should have an equivalent reflowable rendition when possible.
- Give major controls at least 44 by 44 CSS pixels; W3C explains that this larger target is easier to operate on touchscreens in its [target-size guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-enhanced).
- Do not rely only on colour, sound, hover, or fine gestures. Every swipe action also needs a visible button and keyboard-operable equivalent.
- Honour `prefers-reduced-motion`; page turns should become an instant change or subtle dissolve when reduction is requested. The feature is widely supported and maps to iOS’s Reduce Motion setting, as described by [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Reference/At-rules/%40media/prefers-reduced-motion).
- Test VoiceOver, Switch Control, keyboard navigation, zoom, landscape, portrait, and iPad Split View.
- Target [EPUB Accessibility 1.1](https://www.w3.org/TR/epub-a11y-11/) and WCAG 2.2 AA for both web and generated EPUB content.

### Minimal technical shape

Keep each story in structured, presentation-neutral content—Markdown with front matter, or JSON backed by a small schema. Generate:

```text
story source
  -> responsive semantic HTML
  -> PWA offline cache and library manifest
  -> reflowable EPUB 3
  -> optional fixed-layout EPUB rendition
  -> printable PDF/facsimile
```

A web publication manifest can describe title, cover, resources, and `readingOrder` without changing the story HTML. The [Readium manifest specification](https://readium.org/webpub-manifest/) permits HTML, images, audio, and video in that order and provides a future route into Readium-based readers.

For the first prototype, avoid a full EPUB-reading engine in the browser. Render the project’s own clean HTML directly, cache the story shell and selected books through a service worker, and export EPUB during the build. Consider Readium Web only if the site later needs to ingest arbitrary third-party EPUB files.

### MVP acceptance test on an iPad

A useful prototype is one Korean story and one highly illustrated European story. It is ready for child testing when it can:

1. open from a normal HTTPS link and be added to the home screen;
2. download both stories for airplane-mode reading;
3. resume at the last scene after the browser or home-screen app is closed;
4. work in portrait, landscape, and Split View without clipped prose;
5. enlarge type substantially without losing navigation;
6. play narration from a child-initiated tap and highlight the current sentence;
7. remain fully readable with audio, animation, and network access disabled;
8. expose sensible reading order, labels, and image descriptions to VoiceOver;
9. export the same content as a valid reflowable EPUB 3;
10. keep every external source link in an adult-facing colophon rather than the child’s reading path.
