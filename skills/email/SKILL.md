# Email: outreach without the tells

The test that governs everything: **could this sentence appear unchanged in anyone's email to anyone?** If yes, cut it or make it specific to this sender-recipient pair. A cold email earns its read by proving in every sentence that it was written for this one person, about their actual work, by someone with something at stake.

## Shape

Three beats, in order, nothing else:

1. **Context to their work.** One sentence naming the specific thing they made/wrote/found. Verifiable fact, not admiration. State it on their terms: a description they would sign, doing the job they built it for. Their fact deployed as your setup (the cost figure you're about to undercut, the claim you're about to break) is beat 2 wearing beat 1's clothes; save the turn for the turn.
2. **The thing you're showing them.** What it is and why it touches beat 1. State the relevant finding in one sentence. Add a link only as an optional receipt.
3. **Quick actionable ask.** Executable in under a minute, or absent.

Length: 50-125 words total; shorter wins (Hunter.io, 34M cold emails: 20-39 words peaked). If a beat needs two sentences, another beat gives one up.

Cold versus reply. In a cold email, beat 1 is a researched fact about their work. In a reply or a warm thread, beat 1 is the shared context, the ask they made or the call you had, and a one-line acknowledgment of it is legitimate rather than throat-clearing. Either way, cut anything the recipient already knows. Do not disclose or explain back to them something they were already part of, since a disclosure aimed at the public reader is noise to the person who lived it.

## Attention and trust

Assume the recipient triages from the sender, subject, and first two sentences. Most people do not maintain an inbox system that rewards unfamiliar mail, and they do not yet trust an AI with enough private context to filter semantic relevance for them. Earn the open before explaining the project.

AI makes researched personalization cheap. Naming a paper and paraphrasing its abstract no longer proves genuine selection. The credible signal is an object the sender already produced, a filed issue, a result, a receipt, or a precise uncertainty that could change the sender's work. Let the artifact carry trust. Do not compensate with more polished personalization.

Make the first reply feel finite. The recipient should be able to answer in one sentence without accepting a meeting, collaboration, review assignment, or continuing obligation. A brief statement of the sender's goal may explain why the answer matters, but cut it when it turns the reply into an implied commitment to a larger project.

Write for the no-click case. The recipient must understand the observation and answer the ask without opening anything. A link supports provenance for someone who wants to verify the claim. It must never contain the recipient's homework.

Commercial interest. If the sender has one, name it in a plain clause up front ("while researching publishers for an ad exchange I am building"). A bare research framing reads as extraction the moment the interest surfaces, and with a recipient who has nothing to sell, disclosure costs nothing and lands as respect. But name the recipient's actual relation to the work, never pipeline vocabulary: "candidates", "prospects", and "targets" tell the reader they are a row in a spreadsheet.

## Concreteness

The second governing test: **can the recipient check each sentence against something they can see?** Every sentence's grammatical subject is a person, a company, or a product. Abstractions do not act: "the result outlives the company" and "the math ended it" are sentences about nothing, and nobody talks that way. When an abstraction holds the subject slot, find who did what and write that.

Build the email around one checkable fact, never a theme. The sender's shorthand is input vocabulary, not output: "reasoning lift beyond scaling" is a note-to-self, and the email says "your ensemble beat every model inside it." When the brief hands over a theme, find the fact that instances it before drafting a word, and when no such fact exists, say so instead of drafting.

Draft the spoken version first, the sentences the sender would say across a table. The rules in this file compress a natural draft. They cannot rescue a hollow one, and running them on abstractions produces compliant mush.

Failed example, from a sent-adjacent draft: "The ensemble was reasoning lift that scaling alone was not delivering, and that result outlives the company." Passive, runs on, and says nothing specific about the recipient's work, while obeying every punctuation rule below. The concrete version: "Your ensemble beat every model inside it, and that result outlives the company."

## Jargon

Write at the reading level of a text message, not the linked piece. Emails one grade-level simpler get ~36% more replies (Boomerang). The email is the doorway, not the post: ONE term of art maximum, and only the one the recipient owns. Name the one finding needed to understand the question. Do not summarize the whole linked piece. The recipient probably will not click, so the email must carry the premise while the link carries the receipts.

## Tells → fixes

| Tell | Fix |
|---|---|
| "Curious whether/how X…" | Ask directly ("Does X hold up?") or end on the claim. Curiosity is performed; a question is real. |
| "Would love to / I'd love to hear…" | State the ask plainly, or cut. |
| "Thoughts?" / "Let me know what you think" | One specific question answerable in a line, or nothing. |
| "I'd welcome any reaction/feedback" | Name the part you want checked: "Where does the mapping break?" |
| "genuinely / honestly / truly / really" (sincerity markers) | Delete. Sincerity can't be asserted, only shown. If the sentence dies without the adverb, it was hollow. |
| "Happy to share more / happy to chat / hop on a call" | Cut. If they want more they'll ask. Offer only a concrete object you hold ("I have the export if you want it"). |
| "Just reaching out / just wanted to / quick question" | Delete the opener; start with the substance. |
| "I've been following your work / big fan / your X is the strongest I know" | Verifiable fact only: name the piece and what it said. "You wrote X" survives; a superlative doesn't. |
| "Hope this finds you well" | Delete. |
| "resonate(s/d)" | Banned. "Matches", "holds up", "agrees with what you've seen". |
| "reach out" | "write", "email". |
| "No pressure / feel free / no worries if not" | Delete. Granting permission is presumptuous. |
| "Thanks in advance" | Delete (it obligates). Thank only for things already done. |
| Em dashes | 0, as everywhere. |
| Exclamation marks | 0, maybe 1 if the sentence earns it. |
| Throat-clearing that delays the point ("I wanted to write because", "A quick note on", "It is worth saying that", "First, some context") | Cut it and open on the substance. Beat 1 belongs in the first sentence, not the third. |
| Theatrical framing (dedramatize) | Use full sentences instead of dramatic punctuation. A period beats a colon wherever both read the same, so write "The engineering is careful. The sandbox enforces execute-only access." rather than "The engineering gets credit:". Flatten punchy contrasts ("two, where they had twenty-one") and "X, not Y" antitheses into plain statements. Cut emphasis flourishes such as "real credit" or "the kind of thing I flag in others". The facts carry the weight, so the framing should not perform it. |
| Run-on and over-punctuated sentences | Allow at most one punctuation mark other than the closing period in a sentence. One comma, or one colon, or one dash, and then a period. A second mark means two sentences are wearing one coat, so split them. Two independent clauses joined by only a comma are a splice, so use a period instead. Full sentences are the floor, not a license to fuse three of them. |
| "And then" sequencing (beats that merely list, as in this happens and then this happens and also this) | Connect beats by consequence or tension, the Trey Parker rule: every step is a "therefore" or a "but", never an "and then". Once the punctuation rule has chopped the prose into short sentences, this is what keeps them from reading as a flat list. Run the spine inside the three beats, not as extra ones: the substance beat is where a concession sets up the turn, the "but" delivers it, and the "so" pays it off into the ask. A pile of true statements is a list, and a list is skippable. |
| Abstract noun as sentence subject ("the result outlives", "the math ended", "the alignment holds") | Rewrite with the person, company, or product as subject doing a verb. See Concreteness: abstractions do not act. |

## The ask

Include at most one ask, and make it answerable in a single line, or include none at all. Never require a click before the recipient can answer. A strong cold email can end on a statement; the link is supporting evidence, not a second ask. "If a reconciliation course exists, please send me the link" beats "curious if you know of one" because it's an instruction the recipient can execute or ignore, not a mood they're asked to share. Interest-based asks beat meeting requests (304K-email study): ask whether the claim is wrong, not whether they'll get on a call. For an expert recipient, a falsifiable claim they can correct is better bait than a question they can ignore. Never stack asks (read this AND reply AND share it). Imperative asks carry "please": a bare imperative to a stranger reads as a command, and one please per email is politeness rather than begging. One is also the cap, and it belongs on the ask, never on the read ("please see my post" is the beggar's version).

## House format

- Each paragraph is ONE unwrapped line. Hard line breaks inside a paragraph split URLs and have broken sent mail before.
- Gmail drafts and sends use a `text/html` body with simple paragraph markup and clickable links. Other channels stay plain text. Do not use markdown, bullets, or decorative formatting.
- Bare URLs, never link-wrapped, never shortened. Verify no google.com/url or tracking wrapper survives drafting.
- Sign-off: name alone.
- First person claims only what the sender actually did. Delegated or agent work is not "I read/verified" unless they did.
- No borrowed credentials or name-drops. The work carries the email or nothing does.
- Follow-ups only on a new trigger (a public thread, a reply, a shipped thing), never a timer, never same-day.

## Voice

When the sender has past emails that got replies, match their register over every rule here. The evidence base for this file is small next to a sender's own hit: an email that broke a stated no-cold-inbound policy did it with money numbers in every beat, and that register transfers where these rules only constrain.

A line only this sender would write ("wanted to chat with a human for sanity's sake") outranks any rule in this file. The tells table removes the average email; a line like that is the opposite of average. Fix its grammar if broken and otherwise leave it alone.

## Worked example

A reply that hits every rule at once. The beats are labeled; strip the labels before sending.

> Hi Greg,
>
> You asked me to audit a benchmark, but I did not hear back. So I audited MirrorCode. [context: the prior ask, joined by but then so]
>
> The engineering is good, with few errors. But it does not hold up to the marketing promise about program size. I wrote it up: https://june.kim/auditing-mirrorcode [substance: one line of what it found, the turn is a "but", then the link, no recap of the piece]
>
> Please acknowledge it and say whether the findings hold up. [ask: interest-based, answerable in a line, not a meeting request]
>
> June

Fifty-five words. Two commas and one colon across the whole email, each alone in its own sentence. The spine reads "you asked, but I went quiet, so I did one; the work is good, but it fails on size", which is therefore-but, not and-then. It points at the post instead of summarizing it, and it tells Greg nothing he already knows.

## Done test

Read it as the recipient. (1) By the end of sentence one, do they know it was written for them specifically? (2) Does any sentence survive pasting into a different recipient's email unchanged? Cut or specify every sentence that does. (3) Is the ask executable in under a minute, or absent? (4) Is any sentence's subject an abstraction? Put the person or product back in the subject slot. (5) Read it aloud: would the sender say these sentences across a table?

Then run the no-click test. Can the recipient understand the premise and answer without opening a link? Run the obligation test. Does a one-line reply close the loop, or does it accidentally enroll them in a project? Run the costly-signal test. Is the specificity backed by work the sender already did, or could an AI have produced it from the recipient's abstract alone?
